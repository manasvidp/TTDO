      The method

      Five stages. Each one has an exit test.

      Ordinary advice starts with an answer. We start with the decision, and a stage isn't finished because time ran out. It's finished when its test is met.

      01

      Define

      What exactly must be decided?

      We turn a vague concern into a precise decision statement, with the alternatives, an owner and a deadline.

      Exit testYou agree the statement in writing.

      02

      Diagnose

      Where is the uncertainty?

      We separate symptoms from possible causes, map what the team believes and rank the unknowns by how much they could change the call.

      Exit testWe know which unknowns matter.

      03

      Investigate

      What evidence exists?

      We gather internal, customer and market evidence and record every item in an Evidence Ledger, with its source, its grade and the explanations it bears on.

      Exit testEvery high-impact unknown is covered, or the gap is documented.

      04

      Challenge

      Which explanations survive?

      We test every explanation against the same evidence, give weight only to evidence that tells them apart, and deliberately attack the leading answer.

      Exit testA serious alternative has been genuinely tested.

      05

      Decide

      What follows, and how will we know?

      We score the options against criteria agreed in Stage 01, cost the trade-offs and the cost of waiting, and recommend one option with its likelihood, our confidence and how success will be measured.

      Exit testYou can make the decision and defend it.

      How strong is the evidence?

      AStrong
      Direct and complete: system records or a well-designed measure covering the whole population in question.
      BModerate
      Direct but partial: one period, one segment, or a sample large enough to trust but not the whole picture.
      CLimited
      Indirect, or a small sample: a handful of interviews, a proxy measure, an unverified estimate.
      DAnecdotal
      A single account or opinion, unverified. Recorded, never relied on alone.

      Does it tell the explanations apart?

      Evidence counts only if it is more consistent with some explanations than others. A fact that every explanation predicts is recorded but carries no weight. In the decision lab, a card's effect is its direction multiplied by its grade: A counts in full, B for three-quarters, C for half, D for a quarter.

      How likely is the conclusion?

      Almost certain90%+Likely60–90%Roughly even40–60%Unlikely10–40%Remoteunder 10%Cannot assessno basis

      Likelihood is our probability that the conclusion is true. Where we rate several explanations, we say whether they compete for the same place or can all contribute.

      How confident are we in that judgement?

      High
      Several strong, independent sources agree, and the main alternatives have been tested.
      Moderate
      The evidence is credible but has gaps, or rests on one strong source.
      Low
      The evidence is thin, indirect or contested. Treat the judgement as a working hypothesis.

      Likelihood and confidence are separate. "Likely, low confidence" means our best estimate could move a long way with new evidence.

      What kind of claim is it?

      Fact Documented and sourced. Reported Attributed to a named account, not independently confirmed. Our reading Our interpretation of the facts. Unknown What we could not establish, and what would settle it.

      Judging a past decision, we separate what was knowable on the day from what happened afterwards. A good decision can have a bad outcome, and the reverse.

      The promise

      We do not manufacture certainty where the evidence does not support it.

      In every engagement, we will

      • Show the evidence behind every material conclusion
      • State our confidence plainly
      • Present the strongest competing explanation fairly
      • Give a clear recommendation when the evidence allows
      • Name what remains unknown

      We will not

      • Present opinion as a finding
      • Round uncertainty up to certainty
      • Hide evidence that contradicts the recommendation
      • Tell you what you want to hear

      Decision Autopsy™ · Issue 01 · The LEGO Group · decision point 2001

      LEGO said it would refocus. The losses came afterwards.

      The common story says LEGO diversified too far, nearly went bust, and recovered by going back to the brick. That's right about the beginning and the end. The middle doesn't fit.

      LikelyLow confidence

      LEGO publicly diagnosed a loss of focus in 2001, then kept launching lines that weren't built around the brick. Two profitable years made the problem look solved before it was. Confidence is low because the internal evidence that would settle it isn't public.

      1. 1998First loss. Poul Plougmann joins as chief operating officer to lead a turnaround.Reported
      2. 2000Loss of DKK 831m. The annual report says the company "lost focus" and will "refocus on our core business".Fact
      3. 2001Back in profit, led by Harry Potter, Bob the Builder and BIONICLE.Reported
      4. 2002Galidor launches: an action-figure line that departed from the brick system.Fact
      5. 2003Preliminary operating loss of about DKK 1.4bn, reported in January 2004 as the worst in the company's 72-year history.Reported
      6. 2004Plougmann dismissed in January. Net loss of DKK 1.93bn as first reported. Jørgen Vig Knudstorp becomes CEO.Reported
      7. 2005Back in profit after selling the LEGOLAND parks: DKK 505m, as reported in 2006. Later accounts restate the year.Reported

      Figures are as first reported and use different measures (net, operating, preliminary), so they are not directly comparable. LEGO later restated 2004 and 2005.

      The decision

      Whether to refocus on the core brick business after the 2000 loss, or keep expanding into new categories, licences and media.

      What was knowable then

      A loss, a written diagnosis, and then two profitable years. The diagnosis looked confirmed. Our reading

      Alternatives and constraints

      A strict refocus, continued expansion, or a refocus that still left room for new properties. Licensed ranges depended on film release dates LEGO did not control. Our reading

      Competing explanations

      These can all contribute, so each is rated on its own. Refocus announced but not carried out: likely. Licence profits masking the problem: roughly even. Cost base and complexity: roughly even. A shift to electronic toys as the main cause: unlikely.

      Was it a good decision?

      On what was knowable in 2001, refocusing was the right call, and LEGO made it. What failed was carrying it out: the launches of 2002 contradicted the diagnosis of 2001. Our reading

      What we can't establish

      How much of the 2001–02 profit came from licences rather than the classic brick. That internal evidence would decide between the first two explanations. Unknown

      What other businesses can take from it

      An improving number is not evidence that the cause has been fixed. When a fix seems to work, check that the behaviour behind the original problem actually changed.

      Sources and grades: LEGO Annual Report 2000 A · primary · Business Wire report, January 2004, reproduced by LUGNET B · contemporary report · NBC News, 2006 B · contemporary report · Crawford and Robertson, Innovation at the LEGO Group (A), IMD, 2008 B · case study

      When a fix seems to work, how do you know you fixed the right thing?

      Decision Autopsy™ · Issue 02 · Airbus · decision point December 2000

      The A380: a bad decision, or a bad outcome?

      Everyone knows the A380 didn't sell. This investigation starts where the decision did, in December 2000, and asks what Airbus could reasonably have known.

      LikelyModerate confidence

      The A380 rested on one load-bearing assumption: airlines would keep concentrating long-haul traffic through big hubs. It was a reasonable call in 2000, contested at the time, and it held for only a handful of airlines.

      1. 2000Launch approved with 50 firm orders. Airbus forecasts 1,235 passenger aircraft of more than 400 seats over 20 years.Fact
      2. 2005First flight, Toulouse.Fact
      3. 2007Enters service with Singapore Airlines, later than planned.Fact
      4. 2019Emirates cuts its order from 162 to 123. Airbus announces deliveries will end in 2021.Fact
      5. 2021Final delivery, Emirates' 123rd. 251 delivered in all.Fact

      The decision

      Whether to launch a 555-seat double-deck aircraft, betting that airlines would keep routing passengers through congested hub airports.

      What was knowable then

      Airbus cited hub congestion and forecast strong demand for very large aircraft. Boeing argued publicly that airlines would prefer smaller, efficient jets flying direct. The assumption was contested from the start. Reported

      The gap

      Airbus forecast 1,235 passenger aircraft of more than 400 seats over 20 years. It delivered 251 A380s. Its category also counted Boeing 747s, but even with those added, the market fell far short. Our reading

      Competing explanations

      These can all contribute, so each is rated on its own. Efficient twin-engine jets let airlines fly direct: likely, as a major cause. Benchmarking against the 747 rather than the new twins: roughly even. Programme delays cost it its moment: unlikely, as a major cause.

      One detail that cuts the other way

      Emirates alone took 123 of the 251 A380s delivered, about half. Fact Where an airline's whole strategy was a hub, the thesis held. Our reading

      Was it a good decision?

      On what was knowable in 2000, a defensible bet: the assumption was explicit, openly debated and backed by launch orders. The outcome was bad. Whether Airbus set early signals for the assumption failing, and acted on them, isn't visible in public sources. Our reading Unknown

      What other businesses can take from it

      Every big commitment rests on one assumption. The useful question isn't "was the forecast right?" but "what would we expect to see if the assumption were wrong, and when?"

      Sources and grades: CBS News, December 2000 B · contemporary report · Airbus press release, 14 February 2019 A · primary · Aviation News, December 2021 B · trade press · Flight International B · trade press · Cirium analysis B · industry analysis

      Which assumption is your decision resting on, and what would tell you it's wrong?

      Decision Autopsy™ · Issue 03 · Costco Wholesale · decision point 2005

      Costco doesn't sacrifice margin. It moved it.

      Costco is the retailer that leaves money on the table. The facts in that story are mostly right. The word "sacrifice" is the problem.

      LikelyModerate confidence

      The low markup isn't a cost Costco pays for loyalty. It's what members pay an annual fee for. The cap and the fee depend on each other.

      1. 1983First Costco warehouse opens in Seattle.Fact
      2. 2005Jim Sinegal rejects Wall Street calls to raise prices, citing Sears being undercut.Reported
      3. FY05$1.07bn in membership fees against $1.06bn in net income.Fact
      4. 2024US and Canada fees rise from $60 to $65, the first rise since 2017.Fact
      5. FY25$5.3bn in membership fees against about $10.4bn operating income. US and Canada renewal 92.3%.Fact
      6. FY26Fees $5.9bn. US and Canada renewal holds at 92.3%.Fact

      The decision

      In 2005, under pressure from analysts, whether to keep merchandise markups capped, around 14% on branded goods and 15% on Costco's own label by Sinegal's account, or capture the margin customers would probably accept. Reported

      What was knowable then

      Costco's own accounts showed membership fees roughly equal to its entire net income in fiscal 2005. Fact The fee, not the markup, was already where much of the profit came from. Our reading

      Where the profit sits today

      Fee revenue was equivalent to roughly half of operating income in fiscal 2025. That's our arithmetic on reported figures. Our reading

      A number we couldn't settle

      Some published analyses put fees at about three-quarters of operating income. We couldn't reproduce that figure; our calculation from the 10-K gives about half. Costco doesn't report membership profit separately. Unknown

      Competing explanations

      These can all contribute, so each is rated on its own. The fee funds the low prices: likely. The cap stops the company drifting upmarket: likely, as stated intent. Low markups pay on their own through volume: roughly even.

      Was it a good decision?

      On what was knowable in 2005, yes. The fee already matched net income, so raising markups risked the thing members were paying for. Today's renewal rates are consistent with that, though an outcome can't prove a decision was good. Our reading

      What remains unknown

      Whether the caps are still applied strictly today, and how much renewal depends on price rather than habit or convenience. Unknown

      What other businesses can take from it

      Before raising a price, ask what the low price is currently doing for you. Sometimes leaving money on the table is choosing which table it sits on.

      Sources and grades: Costco 10-K, fiscal 2005 A · primary · Costco Annual Report, fiscal 2005 A · primary · Costco 10-K, fiscal 2025 A · primary · Costco 10-K, fiscal 2026 A · primary · Costco Q4 fiscal 2026 results A · primary · Retail TouchPoints, July 2024 B · trade press · Steven Greenhouse, "How Costco Became the Anti-Wal-Mart", The New York Times, July 2005 B · contemporary report

      If you raised prices tomorrow, what would customers stop believing about you?
