      05 · The method

      Five stages. Each one has an exit test.

      Ordinary advice starts with an answer. We start with the decision, and a stage isn't finished because time ran out. It's finished when its test is met.

      01

      Define

      What exactly must be decided?

      We turn a vague concern into a precise decision statement, with the alternatives, an owner and a deadline.

      Exit testYou agree the statement in writing.

      02

      Diagnose

      Where is the uncertainty?

      We separate symptoms from possible causes, map what the team believes and rank the unknowns by how much they could change the call.

      Exit testWe know which unknowns matter.

      03

      Investigate

      What evidence exists?

      We gather internal, customer and market evidence, and grade every claim in an Evidence Ledger.

      Exit testEvery high-impact unknown is covered, or the gap is documented.

      04

      Challenge

      Which explanations survive?

      We test competing explanations, look for contradictions and deliberately attack the leading answer.

      Exit testA serious alternative has been genuinely tested.

      05

      Decide

      What follows, and how will we know?

      We compare the options, cost the trade-offs and give a recommendation with a stated confidence, plus how success should be measured.

      Exit testYou can make the decision and defend it.

      Every claim is graded

      AStrongBModerateCLimitedDAnecdotal

      Every conclusion states its confidence

      Almost certainLikelyRoughly evenUnlikelyRemoteCannot assess

      Most advice hides how sure it is. We put it on the page.

      Decision Autopsy™ · Issue 01 · The LEGO Group, 1998–2005

      LEGO said it would refocus. The losses came afterwards.

      The common story says LEGO diversified too far, nearly went bust, and recovered by going back to the brick. That's right about the beginning and the end. The middle doesn't fit.

      Likely

      LEGO publicly diagnosed over-expansion in 2001, then kept launching categories that weren't built around the brick. Two profitable years made the problem look solved before it was.

      1. 1998First loss. Poul Plougmann brought in to lead a turnaround.Fact
      2. 2000Loss of DKK 831m. The annual report says LEGO "lost focus" and will refocus on its core.Fact
      3. 2001Back in profit, led by Harry Potter, Bob the Builder and BIONICLE.Fact
      4. 2002Galidor launches: an action-figure line that departed from the brick system.Fact
      5. 2003Operating loss of around DKK 1.4bn, the worst in the company's history.Fact
      6. 2004Plougmann dismissed. Loss of DKK 1.93bn, much of it a one-off write-down. Knudstorp becomes CEO.Fact
      7. 2005Profit of DKK 505m after selling the parks and closing a plant.Fact

      The decision

      Whether to refocus on the core brick business after the 2000 loss, or keep expanding into new categories, licences and media.

      What was knowable then

      A loss, a written diagnosis, and then two profitable years. The diagnosis looked confirmed. Our reading

      Alternatives and constraints

      A strict refocus, continued expansion, or a refocus that still left room for new properties. Licensed ranges depended on film release dates LEGO did not control. Our reading

      Competing explanations

      Refocus announced but not carried out: likely. Profits carried by licences, masking the problem: roughly even. Cost base and complexity: roughly even. A shift to electronic toys as the main cause: unlikely.

      What we can't establish

      How much of the 2001–02 profit came from licences versus the classic brick. That internal evidence would decide between the first two explanations. Unknown

      What other businesses can take from it

      An improving number is not evidence that the cause has been fixed. When a fix seems to work, check that the behaviour behind the original problem actually changed.

      Sources: LEGO Annual Report 2000 · Business Newswire, January 2004 · NBC News, 2006 · Crawford and Robertson, Innovation at the LEGO Group (A), IMD, 2008

      When a fix seems to work, how do you know you fixed the right thing?

      Decision Autopsy™ · Issue 02 · Airbus, 2000–2021

      The A380: a bad decision, or a bad outcome?

      Everyone knows the A380 didn't sell. This investigation starts where the decision did, in December 2000, and asks what Airbus could reasonably have known.

      Likely

      The A380 rested on one load-bearing assumption: airlines would keep concentrating long-haul traffic through big hubs. It was reasonable in 2000, and true for only a handful of airlines.

      1. 2000Launch approved with 50 firm orders. Airbus forecasts 1,235 passenger aircraft over 400 seats in 20 years. Boeing forecasts 740.Fact
      2. 2005First flight, Toulouse.Fact
      3. 2007Enters service with Singapore Airlines, later than planned.Fact
      4. 2019Emirates cuts its order from 162 to 123. Airbus announces the end of deliveries.Fact
      5. 2021Final deliveries.Fact

      The decision

      Whether to launch a 555-seat double-deck aircraft, betting that airlines would keep routing passengers through congested hub airports.

      What was knowable then

      Both manufacturers expected growth in very large aircraft and cited hub congestion. They disagreed about how much, not whether. Fact

      The contradiction

      Deliveries fell far below Airbus's forecast. They also fell below Boeing's more cautious one. The pessimist was too optimistic as well. Our reading

      Competing explanations

      Efficient twin-engine jets let airlines fly direct: likely, as the main cause. Benchmarking against the 747 rather than new twins: roughly even. Delays cost the programme its moment: unlikely as the main cause.

      One detail that cuts the other way

      Airbus's 2003 forecast gave the Middle East about 4% of very large aircraft. Emirates and its neighbours ended up taking half. Where an airline's whole strategy was a hub, the thesis held. Fact

      What other businesses can take from it

      Every big commitment rests on one assumption. The useful question isn't "was the forecast right?" but "what would we expect to see if the assumption were wrong, and when?"

      Sources: Flight International · CBS News, December 2000 · Airbus statement, February 2019 · Cirium analysis

      Which assumption is your decision resting on, and what would tell you it's wrong?

      Decision Autopsy™ · Issue 03 · Costco Wholesale, 1983–2026

      Costco doesn't sacrifice margin. It moved it.

      Costco is the retailer that leaves money on the table. The facts in that story are mostly right. The word "sacrifice" is the problem.

      Likely

      The low markup isn't a cost Costco pays for loyalty. It's what members pay an annual fee for. The cap and the fee depend on each other.

      1. 1983First Costco warehouse opens in Seattle.Fact
      2. 2005Jim Sinegal refuses Wall Street pressure to raise prices, citing Sears being undercut.Reported
      3. 2024US and Canada fees rise from $60 to $65, the first rise since 2017.Fact
      4. FY25$5.3bn in membership fees against about $10.4bn operating income. Renewal 92.3%.Fact
      5. FY26Fees $5.9bn. Renewal holds at 92.3% in the US and Canada.Fact

      The decision

      Whether to keep merchandise markups capped, around 14% on branded goods by Sinegal's account, or capture the margin customers would probably accept.

      Where the profit sits

      Fee revenue was equivalent to roughly half of operating income in fiscal 2025. That's our arithmetic on reported figures. Our reading

      A number we couldn't settle

      One published source says fees are about 75% of operating income; our calculation gives about 51%. Costco doesn't report membership profit separately. Unknown

      Competing explanations

      The fee funds the low prices: likely. The cap stops the company drifting upmarket: likely, as stated intent. Low markups pay on their own through volume: roughly even.

      What remains unknown

      Whether the caps are still applied strictly today, and how much renewal depends on price versus habit or convenience. Unknown

      What other businesses can take from it

      Before raising a price, ask what the low price is currently doing for you. Sometimes leaving money on the table is choosing which table it sits on.

      Sources: Costco 10-K, fiscal 2025 · Costco Q4 fiscal 2026 results · Retail TouchPoints, July 2024 · The New York Times, July 2005

      If you raised prices tomorrow, what would customers stop believing about you?
